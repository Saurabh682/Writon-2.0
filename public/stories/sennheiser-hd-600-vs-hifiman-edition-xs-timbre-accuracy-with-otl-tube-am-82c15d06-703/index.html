Sennheiser HD 600 vs HiFiMAN Edition XS: Timbre Accuracy on a Bottlehead Crack-Class OTL Tube Amp

By Nikhil Sen (@nikhil_soundstage) • Specialist: Headphones, IEMs & Audio Gear

The 30-Second Verdict

Core Finding: The Sennheiser HD 600 and HiFiMAN Edition XS represent two fundamentally opposed transducer philosophies. Evaluating them from a high-output-impedance Output Transformerless (OTL) tube amplifier—exemplified by the Bottlehead Crack—introduces an electrical mismatch that heavily shapes the outcome:

  • OTL Synergy Winner: Sennheiser HD 600 (its 300Ω load naturally mates with high-impedance voltage-swing circuits, where an amplifier like the Crack delivers ~10V into 300Ω).
  • Spatial & Technical Winner: HiFiMAN Edition XS (wide spatial presentation and linear sub-bass extension down to 20 Hz, but requires a low-output-impedance solid-state amplifier to deliver its intended performance).
  • Watch Before Buying: An 18Ω planar magnetic headphone on a 120Ω OTL tube amplifier encounters significant signal voltage attenuation and demands high current from an output stage optimized for high-voltage swings into high-impedance loads.

Evidence Basis: This technical assessment synthesizes independently measured laboratory frequency response curves from RTINGS.com, comparative acoustical evaluations from Headphones.com, official manufacturer specifications from Sennheiser and HiFiMAN, and electrical circuit analysis of the classic Bottlehead Crack 120Ω OTL topology.

1. The Specific Question: Electrical Matching Before Acoustic Evaluation

When audiophiles debate timbre accuracy, discussions often focus solely on subjective impressions of "warmth" or "air." But when comparing the Sennheiser HD 600 against the planar magnetic HiFiMAN Edition XS on an OTL tube amplifier, the primary question is fundamentally electrical:

Does the HD 600's reference vocal timbre retain its advantage over the Edition XS's planar resolution when driven by a Bottlehead Crack-class high-output-impedance OTL amplifier, or does the amplifier's 120Ω source impedance place the planar headphone outside its intended operating window?

An Output Transformerless (OTL) tube amplifier omits the iron-core output transformer to achieve high transient speed and minimal phase shift. Without a transformer to step down high plate voltages into high-current low-impedance drive, cathode-follower circuits like the classic Bottlehead Crack present a high source impedance: typically 120Ω (as specified by Bottlehead, which notes that the Crack is optimized for high-impedance headphones and that lower-impedance loads are not optimal). While certain OTL designs employ heavy parallel tube banks or dedicated feedback loops to achieve lower source impedances, the 120Ω topology represents the standard design archetype encountered in this class.

For the 300Ω dynamic voice coil of the HD 600, a 120Ω source yields a damping factor of approximately 2.5 ($300 / 120$)—a stable, predictable operating range for moving-coil headphones on tube gear. For the 18Ω planar driver of the Edition XS, the nominal electrical damping ratio drops to approximately 0.15 ($18 / 120$). Planar magnetic transducers feature flat impedance curves across the audible band and are mechanically and acoustically damped by diaphragm tensioning and symmetrical magnet structures rather than relying purely on electrical back-EMF braking. However, placing an 18Ω resistive load across a 120Ω output stage creates severe voltage attenuation and forces the amplifier's cathode follower into high current delivery where distortion rises.

3. Physical & Electrical Grounding: Why High-Z OTL Amps Favor 300Ω Over 18Ω

To understand why perceived timbre and dynamics shift across these headphones, one must examine how signal voltage divides across the load:

$\text{Damping Factor} = \frac{Z_{\text{headphone}}}{Z_{\text{amplifier}}}$

$V_{\text{load}} = V_{\text{source}} \times \frac{Z_{\text{load}}}{Z_{\text{out}} + Z_{\text{load}}}$

On a Bottlehead Crack-class amplifier with an output impedance ($Z_{\text{out}}$) of 120Ω:

  1. The Sennheiser HD 600 Interaction: The HD 600 exhibits a nominal impedance of 300Ω, with a voice-coil resonance peak reaching roughly 500Ω around 100 Hz in independent impedance-versus-frequency measurements. In a voltage-divider network with a 120Ω source, voltage division at 300Ω delivers: $\frac{300}{120 + 300} = \frac{300}{420} \approx 0.714\text{ (a } -2.92\text{ dB attenuation)}$ At the 500Ω resonance peak around 100 Hz: $\frac{500}{120 + 500} = \frac{500}{620} \approx 0.806\text{ (a } -1.87\text{ dB attenuation)}$ This difference yields a modest bass-region lift of approximately 1.05 to 1.1 dB in the 80–120 Hz zone ($20 \log_{10}(0.806 / 0.714) \approx 1.05\text{ dB}$). On a Bottlehead Crack, this interaction imparts a gentle fullness to the HD 600's lower registers while leaving its linear 300 Hz–3 kHz midrange unaffected.

  2. The HiFiMAN Edition XS Interaction: Planar magnetic drivers present an essentially flat, resistive impedance curve across the audible spectrum; the Edition XS remains steady at 18Ω from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, meaning amplifier output impedance does not alter its frequency response shape. However, placing an 18Ω load across a 120Ω source absorbs only about 13% of the amplifier's signal voltage ($\frac{18}{120 + 18} = \frac{18}{138} \approx 0.130$, an attenuation of $-17.7\text{ dB}$), while demanding heavy current ($I = \frac{V}{R}$). To reach normal listening volume, the volume control must be turned up substantially, pushing the output tube's cathode follower toward its current-delivery limits. On high-amplitude musical peaks, this risks soft clipping, harmonic compression, and degraded low-frequency impact.

A Bottlehead Crack-class high-output-impedance OTL is simply outside the Edition XS's intended design envelope.

A. Vocal Tone: Chest Resonance vs. Midrange Recession
  • Sennheiser HD 600: An established reference standard. In independent laboratory acoustic measurements from RTINGS.com, the HD 600 tracks diffuse-field neutral target curves across the 300 Hz–3 kHz band with exceptional consistency. Male and female vocal fundamentals (150–300 Hz) transition directly into upper-midrange ear-gain harmonics (1–3 kHz) without artificial bumps or scoops. Reviewers across Headphones.com consistently highlight this region as the HD 600's defining strength: vocal timbre sounds direct, uncolored, and free of chestiness or nasal pinch.
  • HiFiMAN Edition XS: Measurements from RTINGS.com document a subtle 1.5–2 kHz recession in the Edition XS's frequency response curve. Acoustically, this slight dip positions vocal fundamentals slightly further back within the virtual acoustic field. While vocal clarity remains high and free of sibilance, vocal bodies present as slightly leaner and less forward than on the HD 600, trading vocal intimacy for an expanded sense of ambient space.
B. Acoustic Piano: Hammer Attack vs. Harmonic Decay
  • Sennheiser HD 600: Acoustic piano demonstrates the value of the HD 600's linear midrange. Because the frequency response avoids sharp peaks across 2–6 kHz, piano strikes maintain natural soundboard resonance and realistic wood decay across middle octaves.
  • HiFiMAN Edition XS: The ultra-thin planar diaphragm's rapid transient response resolves the leading edge of hammer strikes with high speed and separation. However, technical reviews note that planar transient speed combined with the 1.5–2 kHz dip gives piano decays a slightly drier, more analytical acoustic character compared to the HD 600.
C. Cymbals & High Percussion: Natural Brass vs. Planar Sheen
  • Sennheiser HD 600: Sennheiser rolls off treble smoothly above 10 kHz. In listening evaluations, this smooth slope renders brass cymbals and high-hat transients with natural metallic body and harmonic decay, avoiding splashiness, excessive sizzle, or piercing edge.
  • HiFiMAN Edition XS: Laboratory sweeps show a noticeable frequency elevation between 8 kHz and 12 kHz. This air-band rise enhances spatial cues, reverb trails, and micro-detail, but it can introduce a faint metallic sheen or extra bite on aggressively mastered percussion tracks.
D. Double Bass & Lower Strings: OTL Mid-Bass Lift vs. Planar Sub-Bass Extension
  • Sennheiser HD 600: Driven by a Bottlehead Crack-class amplifier, the HD 600's voice-coil resonance interaction imparts a modest, woody fullness to acoustic upright bass. However, physical driver limits mean fundamental notes below 50 Hz roll off steeply.
  • HiFiMAN Edition XS: On a low-impedance amplifier, the Edition XS extends flat down to 20 Hz with minimal sub-bass attenuation, delivering structural resonance and acoustic weight that conventional moving-coil drivers roll off. On a 120Ω OTL, however, current starvation reduces low-end authority and muddies bass definition.

5. Transducer Capability vs. Amplifier Synergy

To reach an honest verdict, one must untangle transducer capability from amplifier pairing:

  • On a Bottlehead Crack-Class High-Output-Impedance OTL: The Sennheiser HD 600 demonstrates strong electrical and acoustic synergy. The 120Ω output impedance imparts a modest mid-bass lift that balances the headphone's natural low-end roll-off while allowing its linear midrange tracking to shine. The Edition XS on this same amplifier sounds dynamically compressed and hollowed out due to voltage attenuation and current limits.
  • On a Low-Impedance Solid-State Amp (< 1Ω, e.g., Topping L30 II or JDS Atom 2): The comparison shifts. Given sufficient current into 18Ω, the Edition XS delivers wide transient contrast, sharp instrument separation, and an expansive, dimensional soundstage. Its midrange remains slightly leaner than the HD 600, but its advantages in layering and sub-bass depth become apparent.

7. The Actionable Buying Verdict

  • Choose the Sennheiser HD 600 if:

    • Your music library centers on acoustic genres: vocal jazz, chamber music, classical piano, and folk.
    • Your primary headphone amplifier is a Bottlehead Crack-class high-output-impedance OTL tube amplifier (such as the Bottlehead Crack, DarkVoice 336SE, or Feliks Audio) or a suitable high-voltage headphone amplifier.
    • You prioritize lifelike vocal timbre and long-session listening fatigue resistance above spatial grandeur.

  • Choose the HiFiMAN Edition XS if:

    • You listen extensively to orchestral arrangements, electronic music, modern rock, or ambient genres where sub-bass extension and wide soundstage layering take priority.
    • You already own—or plan to buy—a dedicated solid-state amplifier with high current delivery and an output impedance under 1Ω (such as the Schiit Magni+, Topping L30 II, or JDS Atom 2).
    • You understand that running an 18Ω planar off a Bottlehead Crack-class high-output-impedance OTL amplifier places the headphone outside its operating envelope and degrades its performance.
