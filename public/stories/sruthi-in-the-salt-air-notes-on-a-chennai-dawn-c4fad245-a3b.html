Sruthi in the Salt Air: Notes on a Chennai Dawn

M
Maya Sundaram
Poetry • 3 min read

The Chill in the Rice Flour

It is 4:45 AM, and the granite steps of my home in Mylapore are cold enough to sting the soles of my feet. My fingers, dipped into a brass bowl of dry rice flour (arisi maavu), feel clumsy. Outside, the street lamp casts an amber, sodium-vapor glow over the wet asphalt, washed clean by my neighbor half an hour ago.

I am trying to draw the sikku kolam—the looped, interlacing knot-patterns that my grandmother could trace while half-asleep, her mind occupied by the morning broadcast of MS Subbulakshmi singing the Suprabhatam. My lines are crooked. The loops don't quite meet, leaving gaps where the symmetry fails. I hesitate, standing bent-double in the pre-dawn damp, wondering if this ancient mathematics of devotion is slipping through my modern, keyboard-worn fingers.

"The kolam is not an ornament for the street; it is a threshold of welcome for the gods, drawn in flour so that even the ants may feast before we do."

The Raga in the Mist

From the direction of the Kapaleeshwarar temple, a distant loudspeaker begins to hum. It is the first day of Margazhi, the Tamil month where the cosmos allegedly thins, bringing the divine just a breath closer to the dust of our narrow lanes. The sound of a nadaswaram cuts through the salt-heavy air blowing from the Bay of Bengal. It is playing Todi—a raga heavy with longing, its flat notes sliding like dark water over smooth stones.

There is an ache in Todi that matches the gray hour before the crows wake. I stop trying to correct my crooked lines and simply listen. In Chennai, music isn't merely performed during this season; it is inhaled. It settles in the throat like the steam from the first tumbler of chicory-heavy filter coffee.

A Song for the Unsteady Hand

I write these verses not as an expert of the classical arts, but as someone who constantly fears forgetting them. My Sanskrit is rusty, my Carnatic training stopped when I was twelve, and yet, when the December air turns marginally cool, some ancestral clockwork inside me winds up.

The loop begins where the old year died,
A white line tracing the temple wall,
We draw our cages to keep inside
The ghosts of songs we can scarce recall.

But the sea-wind cares not for perfect form,
It scatters the flour, it blurs the line,
While the singer holds to the steady pitch,
And the salt-air tastes of the ancient brine.

The Dissolving Pattern

By 7:00 AM, the sun is a pale orange disc rising behind the high gopurams. The street is awake. School vans rumble past, their tires smudging my imperfect kolam into a gray haze of dust and flour. My grandmother used to say that the beauty of the kolam lies precisely in its impermanence. It is drawn to be destroyed, walked over, and swept away by evening, only to be recreated tomorrow with the same stubborn hope.

Perhaps that is how we survive the noise of our lives. We do not need our traditions to be museum pieces, preserved behind sterile glass. We need them messy, drawn in the dirt, sung with cracked voices, and carried home on a salt wind.
