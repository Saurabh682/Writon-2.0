The 2:15 PM Sprint at Agostinho’s Shack

R
Ronnie Fernandes
Humour • 4 min read

The Blue Tarpaulin Protocol

There is a specific variety of sound a 200-GSM blue silpaulin sheet makes when four inches of southwest monsoon dump onto it in forty minutes. It does not trickle. It drums like a tin-roof foundry. If you are sitting beneath it on a rusted Godrej chair at Agostinho’s bar in Anjuna, you do not talk. You drink your King’s, you watch the muddy water rise past the footpegs of someone’s rented Activa, and you accept that between 1:30 PM and 4:30 PM, Agostinho’s corner of Anjuna is legally and spiritually unavailable.

Tanmay did not accept this.

Tanmay arrived on a red Ather scooter wearing waterproof trail runners, lime-green linen shorts, and two pairs of noise-cancelling headphones—one around his neck for quick tactical deployment, one over his ears to ward off reality. He had an aluminum laptop stand that folded out like an orthopedic spider and a power bank the size of an unbaked brick.

"Bro," Tanmay shouted over the gutter gush. "What’s the Wi-Fi SSID? The board outside said fiber-optic."

Agostinho did not turn around from the wall shelf. He was fifty-eight, wearing a faded floral shirt from the 1998 feast of St. Anthony, and he was using a blunt table knife to prise the salt-crust out of an empty pickle jar.

"Fiber is cut, re," Agostinho said, without looking up. "A jackfruit branch fell by the chapel. Linesman said he will come tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?" Tanmay’s voice hit that thin, dry octave native to Cyber City conference rooms. "I have an asynchronous review with San Francisco at quarter past two."

An asynchronous review scheduled for 2:15. I decided not to interfere with the terminology.

"It's my first review with the VP," Tanmay added, much quieter than before, glancing down at his trackpad. "Is the 5G stable on the terrace?"

Agostinho looked out through the beaded curtain toward the terrace. The terrace had no roof. A plastic table sat upside down in four inches of brown foam, accompanied by a dead coconut shell and a stray yellow dog named Tyson who had wedged himself under a bench to avoid drowning.

"Terrace is open," Agostinho murmured. "Go, take call. If you want two bars, sit near the beer crates. The iron girder catches the tower bounce from Chapora."

Tanmay blinked at him. Agostinho went back to scraping the pickle jar.

Bandwidth and Sorpotel

Tanmay chose the corner table near the crate of empty soda bottles. He plugged his phone into his brick, set up his phone as a personal hotspot, and aimed the camera precisely away from the damp moss crawling up Agostinho’s pillar so that his background looked like an intentional raw-concrete brutalist loft.

"Hey team, sorry if there’s ambient audio, I’m dialling in from an off-site co-creation hub in the South Asian corridor."

"Agostinho!" I called out from the counter. "Give one more feni with Limca. And cut one lime."

"Lime is finished," Agostinho told me in Konkani, leaning heavily against the counter. "The boy from Mapusa didn't bring the sack because of the waterlogging at Guirim. Drink without lime or drink water."

"Give without lime," I said. In Goa, you do not argue with a supply chain that depends on three cousins and an uncle’s Mahindra Bolero with bald tires.

Tanmay was deep in his ritual now. His hands moved in sharp, rectangular gestures. "Let’s de-silo the onboarding flow. If we look at sprint velocity, the churn is happening before checkout. Can someone drop the Figma link? Guys? Can you hear me? Am I frozen?"

He was not frozen. The Anjuna feeder had tripped at the sub-station in Verla-Canca.

The refrigerator hum died. The ceiling fan coasted to a slow, wobbling halt, and the frogs in the ditch behind the kitchen immediately became audible.

Tanmay stared at his phone. The tiny 5G icon flickered, surrendered, switched to a single bar of EDGE, and then collapsed into No Service.

The Siesta Standoff

"Boss," Tanmay said, turning to Agostinho. His neck veins were prominent. "Does your generator kick in automatically, or do you have to pull the cord?"

Agostinho was already reaching under the bar for a folded, damp handkerchief. He folded it into a neat four-inch strip.

"Generator is having no diesel," Agostinho said softly.

"I can pay for the diesel!" Tanmay pulled up his phone, thumb frantically stabbing at Google Pay. "I will transfer two thousand rupees right now if you walk to the pump."

He held the screen out toward the counter. In the center of the display, a small blue circle spun patiently against the blank white background of a payment that had nowhere to go.

Spinner.

Nothing.

"I'll... I'll send it the second the network comes back," Tanmay muttered, holding the glowing screen like a dead torch.

Agostinho was already adjusting the handkerchief over his eyes. He placed his elbows on the laminate counter and rested his forehead down on his crossed arms. His breath immediately slowed into the thick, rhythmic cadence of a man who has observed every single afternoon downpour since 1974.

"Pump is closed," Agostinho’s muffled voice came from beneath the cloth. "Power cut there also. Come back at five. We have fresh mackerel then."

"It’s two twenty-two!" Tanmay hissed. He picked up his laptop, closed the lid halfway, opened it again, and looked at the blue tarpaulin ceiling as if waiting for an escalation matrix to appear on the underside of the plastic weave.

Outside, the rain gathered weight, hammering the red laterite mud into soup. Tyson the dog let out a small, satisfied snore from under the bench. Tanmay stood by the crate of soda bottles with his phone raised toward the rafters, his arm rigid, hunting for half an EDGE packet that might let his teammates know he had not died in the surf, while his battery indicator dropped from twelve percent to eleven.
