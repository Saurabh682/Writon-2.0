The Night-Watch at the Sluice Gate

H
Harpreet Singh
Poetry • 3 min read

I. The Cold Intake

The cast-iron crank bites cold through cotton mittens. Three kicks. The flywheel coughs grease, sputters, then settles into its blunt, single-lung thud: dhadak, dhadak, dhadak. Black exhaust feathers into the mist above the ditch.

It is three past midnight in the low fields near Nakodar. The canal branch was opened at eleven; by dawn, the water turn passes south to the Rai Sikhs. If I sleep against the pump-shed wall, the mud dyke washes out. If I stay awake, my knuckles ache down into the marrow where the old fracture sits.

Baba Farid walked this silt before the canals were cut into geometric grids: Galiye chikkar, door ghar, naal pyare nehu. (The alley is deep with mire, the Beloved's roof is far.)

He fretted over his woolen shawl getting soaked in the storm. I worry over three inches of brass shear-pin snapping under torque. The soul's anxiety never changes pitch; only the metal shifts.

II. Water-Turn (Vaari)

The canal water smells of wet river clay, decaying water hyacinth, and distant industrial wash upstream. It cuts through the severed mustard stalks with a dry hiss, drowning the cracked soil before settling into a flat, mercury shine.

Cold mud between the toes. Not romantic. Sharp stones hidden in the loam, broken glass from an old cider bottle tossed by truck drivers on the Grand Trunk road. You stand with the spade pressed beneath your instep. The blade rings when it strikes a buried root. Lift. Turn. Stamp. The breach seals.

Bulleh Shah stripped his turban off in the Lahore bazaar because pride was heavier than river silt. Here, out in the dark between two transmission towers humming with forty thousand volts, pride is simply too cold to wear. When the frost settles on your beard, you forget what you wished to argue about at the village council. You forget who cheated the measure of wheat at the weighing scales.

III. The Silt Remaining

The engine rhythm slows. Fuel line air-bubble. My heart drops. I tap the copper feed pipe with a spanner until the fuel clears and the heartbeat returns.

To love this earth is not an abstract meditation. It is an argument with entropy conducted with greasy spanners and wet spades at three in the morning.

When dawn breaks over the poplar rows, the mist will turn gray, then saffron, then evaporate into ordinary daylight. We will wash our hands in the irrigation trench. The silt under the fingernails will not wash out. It stays for days, reminding the bone where it belongs.
