The Bevel of the Qalam

F
Farhan Akhtar Kazmi
Short Stories • 3 min read

The Chisel of the Knife

The bone-handled folding knife clicked open. Three scrapings. A curl of dry river reed spiraled onto the green baize cloth covering his knees.

Mohsin did not look at the clock on the archway of Urdu Bazaar, though its second hand ticked with the harshness of a dropped nickel. He held the hollow nib of the bamboo qalam up against the naked filament bulb hanging from the wooden ceiling beam. The bevel was forty-five degrees. Too steep, and the sheen—the sweeping belly of the letter noon—would feather like wet soot along its underside. Too shallow, and the stroke dried before the ink completed the bow of an alif.

Outside the six-foot wooden shopfront, the evening rush from Jama Masjid’s Gate One pushed past: the tang of boiling mustard oil from the fry-stalls, the diesel reek of cycle-rickshaws fighting for turning clearance, the mechanical chatter of an offset printing unit two doors down churning out three thousand fluorescent wedding cards an hour.

"Ustad-ji," a voice broke the rhythm.

A young man in a synthetic tracksuit stood in the lane, holding a folded slip of glossy paper. He had the sharp, impatient posture of someone whose motorcycle engine was still idling at the corner of Chitli Qabr.

"The clerk at the registrar said the registry clerk won't accept digital prints for the ancestral wakf deed," the boy said. He did not step onto the wooden threshold. "Needs the original title deed line in nastaliq. Seven lines. How long?"

Mohsin dipped the reed into the brass dawaat.

Inside the inkwell, a nest of raw unspun silk—the likka—drank the lampblack fluid, preventing the pen from pulling up too heavy a droplet. It was the same ink his father had prepared in clay crocks during the hot winds of June: soot scraped from copper lamps, gum arabic from the acacia tree, crushed gallnuts, and five drops of bitter clove oil to keep the paper from souring in the monsoon damp.

"An hour," Mohsin said.

"An hour? The computer shop at Daryaganj prints a page in ten seconds."

"Then go to Daryaganj," Mohsin replied. His voice was neither angry nor grand; it had the worn, flat texture of the sandstone steps below the mosque. "A laser prints ink onto the skin of the paper. It sits there like dry flour. The reed splits the surface. It drives the iron into the fiber. When your grandson disputes the courtyard boundary forty years from now, the toner will have flaked away in the tin trunk. The reed will remain."

The young man hesitated, looked back toward the noisy lane, and pulled a crumpled five-hundred-rupee note from his pocket. He laid it on the corner of the low desk.

Mohsin did not touch the paper money. He smoothed a fresh leaf of handmade wasli paper across his wooden writing board. He adjusted the glass inkwell closer to his thumb, took a shallow breath, and rested his wrist against the edge of the wood.

The nib touched the paper with a soft, paper-cutting hiss. Zzzzt.

The black line swelled thick, curved with the slow deliberate weight of iron, and tapered to a whisper-thin hairline as his thumb rotated the reed. For forty minutes, the market dissolved into that narrow scrap of light: the smell of cloves, the scrape of steel knife against reed, and the steady, quiet discipline of an alphabet that refused to surrender its geometry to speed.
