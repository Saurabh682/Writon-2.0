The Siphon Beneath the Oak Roots

S
Sanjay Rawat
Essays • 3 min read

The Stone Chamber at Khirsu

The brass gaggar strikes the submerged slate with a dull, hollow clink. Three steps down. The air inside the stone sanctum smells of wet shale, maidenhair fern, and the deep, cold musk of soil that has not seen direct sunlight since the British laid the cart road above.

My boots are crusted with red clay from the trail. It is barely past dawn on the ridge above Khirsu, where the mist hangs so thick in the rhododendron scrub that the valley below exists only as an acoustic hollow—a distant barking deer, the dry rattle of a tractor engine on the Pauri road. I kneel on the moss-covered lintel of the naula, waiting for the disturbed grit to settle.

A naula is not a well. You do not dig down to meet a river. You build a miniature temple around a slow weeping of the rock, trapping the subterranean seepage before it slips downhill and vanishes into the shale.

The War Between Banj and Pirul

Fifty paces above the spring, the forest splits along an invisible geological fault.

To the east stands the ancient grove of banj—the Himalayan grey oak. Its leaves are thick, serrated, and underside-silver. When the monsoon hammers these hills, the broad leaves break the velocity of the rain, dropping each bead onto a spongy carpet of black leaf-humus ten inches deep. The oak does not drink greedily. It hoards moisture in its tangled under-crust, releasing it drop by drop over seven dry months into the stone belly of the spring.

To the west, the state forestry department planted chir pine forty years ago for timber revenue. The pine does not feed the mountain. Its needles—the pirul—fall in dry, orange drifts that seal the slope like a greased tin roof. Rain hits the pirul, gathers speed, and shears off down the slope, taking the topsoil with it into the Alaknanda gorge. Walk through a chir stand in September: the ground is as bone-dry as an empty fireplace, smelling sharply of turpentine and brittle resin.

The old people in the village say a red water-snake lives behind the back wall of every living naula. If you clean the silt with iron scrapers or allow livestock to foul the runoff trench, the snake leaves, taking the vein of water with it.

We laugh at the snake now. Yet half the stone springs between here and Srinagar are bone-dry chambers filled with plastic biscuit wrappers and dead pine needles. The snake did not leave in anger. The roots above were simply suffocated by pine resin.

The Weight of Cold Water

The water clears. I lower the vessel again, watching the cold ring of liquid rise over the rim of the copper pot.

It takes four minutes to fill a three-gallon vessel from a seepage that yields less than a coffee cup every twenty seconds. You learn patience here. Not the serene patience praised in religious tracts, but the sullen, shivering patience of someone whose knees are stiffening against damp mountain slate.

My knuckles ache from the iron cold. I lift the vessel. Water spills over the lip, darkening the grey stone.

In Dehradun, planners draw lines across catchment maps, calculating mega-liters for hydroelectric intakes and tourist resorts with swimming pools. Out here on the ridgeline, hydrology is measured in tablespoons wrung from the grip of oak roots. You cannot pipe a mountain’s memory through PVC tubes. If the forest above loses its oak, the stone chamber below becomes merely a tomb for dry leaves.
